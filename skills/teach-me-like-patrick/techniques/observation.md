# Technique: Observation (the look)

_Character-free technique note. Lessons and scenes link here; personas add the voice. Source keys refer to `references/sources.md`._

## What it is

Noticing small, concrete details about a person (clothes, hands, posture, objects, wear patterns, what they touch, what they avoid) and turning them into **hypotheses** about their life, habits and current state. The skill is two halves: *seeing more* and *guessing well*. Most people fail at the first half: they look but don't register.

## How to do it

1. **Scan in a fixed order** so nothing gets skipped: head → face → hands → clothes → shoes → what they carry → how they move → what's around them (their space, their phone, their bag).
2. **Name details, not conclusions.** "Ink on the side of the right little finger" before "writer". "Fresh tan line where a ring was" before "divorced".
3. **Ask "what makes this?"** for each detail. Wear, stains, calluses, fading, mismatches and recent changes are the richest clues because they are *caused* by repeated behaviour.
4. **Generate two or three explanations** for every clue. A tan line could be divorce, a gym habit, a lost ring, or a ring being resized.
5. **Look for convergence.** One clue is a guess; three clues pointing the same way is a read.
6. **Hold it as a hypothesis and test it** with a light question or statement, then update. Being wrong cheaply is part of the skill.
7. **Note what's *missing* or *changed*:** the person who always wears a watch and isn't today; the confident talker who goes quiet on one topic.

## Science check

- **Works (with limits):** brief observations ("thin slices") predict some outcomes with moderate accuracy (Ambady & Rosenthal 1992, 1993). Strangers judged some traits above chance from someone's bedroom or office (openness and conscientiousness best; Gosling et al. 2002) and from full-body photos (extraversion, self-esteem; Naumann et al. 2009). They did better when people stood naturally, so posture and expression carry real signal. Accuracy is modest, and many traits can't be read this way.
- **Works:** deliberate attention changes what you notice. Untrained attention misses big things: about half the viewers in the famous "gorilla" study never saw the gorilla (Simons & Chabris 1999), and 7 of 15 people didn't notice the stranger they were talking to had been swapped for someone else (Simons & Levin 1998). A fixed scanning routine fixes much of this.
- **TV exaggeration:** one glance yielding a full biography with certainty. Real reads are probabilistic, need several converging clues, and are often wrong on the first try.
- **Myth:** judging character from a face. Face-based first impressions are fast and widely shared, but mostly inaccurate (Todorov et al. 2015). Read *behaviour and evidence* (wear, habits, choices), not features.
- **Myth:** that any single detail "means" one thing. Clues have base rates and alternatives.

## Common mistakes

- Jumping to the conclusion and then seeing only confirming clues (confirmation bias).
- Reading stereotypes instead of evidence (accent, caste, religion, gender, body size). That is prejudice, not observation; it is also usually wrong.
- Saying the read out loud to the person in a way that embarrasses them.

## Ethics line

Observe to understand and connect, not to expose. Never announce private inferences (health, relationships, money, religion, sexuality) about a real person to them or others.

## Drill ideas

- **60-second stranger:** pick someone in a café or a photo; list 10 details in 60 s, then 3 hypotheses, each with its supporting clues.
- **What changed?** Look at a scene or photo for 20 s, then look at a modified version and spot the changes.
- **Friend check:** make three observations-based guesses about a friend's day; ask them; score hits and misses honestly.
