# Technique: Baselines and lies

_Character-free technique note. Source keys refer to `references/sources.md`. This is the note where TV and science disagree most; keep the science check prominent in every lesson that uses it._

## The honest headline

**No body-language "tell" proves a lie.** Not looking away, not touching the nose, not crossed arms, not a flash of an expression. Across 206 studies and about 24,000 judges, people averaged about 54% accuracy, barely better than a coin flip, and experts did little better (Bond & DePaulo 2006). Of 158 behaviours studied, most had no link or only a weak link to lying (DePaulo et al. 2003). Training people on body-language "symptoms" made them *more confident but less accurate*: about 46% vs 56% for untrained people (Kassin & Fong 1999). The problem isn't that people look at the wrong signs; the signal itself is very weak (Hartwig & Bond 2011). What *does* help is mostly about **what people say** and **how you ask**.

**The one rule that beats gut feeling:** judge an account by **how much specific, checkable detail** it has (who, where, when, what exactly). Intuitive "use every cue" judgments were at chance; people told to use only this cue reliably did better (Verschuere et al. 2023). Content-based analysis of statements also separates true from invented accounts above chance (Oberlader et al. 2016).

## What to do instead

### 1. Baseline first
Watch how the person behaves when relaxed and talking about neutral, easily verifiable things: their normal speed, eye contact, fidgeting, detail level and voice. Every person is different. A naturally fidgety person who fidgets tells you nothing.

### 2. Watch for change, then ask why
A shift from baseline (sudden stillness, a change in detail level, a pause before one specific answer) is a **flag to look closer**, not proof. Stress, embarrassment and fear of not being believed cause the same changes in honest people.

### 3. Ask better questions (this is where the real edge is)
- **Open, story-first questions:** "Walk me through your evening from the moment you left work." Truthful accounts tend to be richer in checkable detail.
- **Unexpected questions** that a prepared liar didn't rehearse: spatial ("draw the room"), sequence, sensory detail. When two people lie about the same event, their answers to unexpected questions match each other less (Vrij, Leal et al. 2009).
- **Increase cognitive load,** e.g. asking for the story in reverse order. Lying is mentally harder, and extra load can make it show (Vrij et al. 2008). Honest note: how big this advantage is remains debated (Levine et al. 2018; Mac Giolla & Luke 2021).
- **Strategic use of evidence:** don't reveal what you know. Let the story commit first, then check it against your facts. In a police-training study, interviewers who did this caught liars about 85% of the time vs about 56% for untrained ones (Hartwig et al. 2006). It only works when you actually *have* facts.
- **Check consistency** with known facts and with their own earlier statements, not with your gut feeling.

### 4. Hold the conclusion loosely
End with "this doesn't add up; I need more information", not "you're lying".

## Science check

- **Works best:** checking against evidence and judging by detail (strategic use of evidence; the detail rule). This is where the strongest evidence is.
- **Works, more modestly:** unexpected questions and cognitive load. They help in studies, but the size of the gain is debated, and none of it gives certainty.
- **Works:** baselining as a habit, because it stops you from misreading someone's normal style. Body-language books (e.g. Navarro & Karlins 2008) are best read as *comfort/discomfort* signals to check against a baseline, not lie detectors; Navarro himself says no single behaviour means lying.
- **TV exaggeration:** a detective spotting the lie from one glance or one micro-expression. In frame-by-frame analysis, micro-expressions showed up in only about 2% of expressions, were always partial, and also appeared in genuine emotions (Porter & ten Brinke 2008). People trained with a micro-expression tool did no better than untrained people, slightly below chance (Jordan et al. 2019).
- **Myth:** "liars look away", "liars look up-right", "crossed arms = defensive lie". Not supported. Eye-movement direction specifically was tested in three studies and did not distinguish liars from truth-tellers (Wiseman et al. 2012).
- **Myth:** "93% of communication is nonverbal" (Mehrabian's 7-38-55). The original studies only covered judging *like vs dislike* from single words said in a clashing tone; Mehrabian himself says the formula doesn't apply to ordinary communication.
- **Also true:** humans default to believing others (Truth-Default Theory, Levine 2014). Most communication is honest, so that default is usually healthy, not a flaw to train away. Lie detection improves mainly through context, evidence and good questions.

## Ethics line

Don't interrogate your partner, friends or employees, and don't accuse someone based on body language. Use this to ask better questions and to avoid being fooled, not to play detective on people's private lives.

## Drill ideas

- **Baseline game:** the agent describes a person's normal behaviour, then a moment; the learner says whether it's a change worth noticing and gives 3 innocent explanations.
- **Two truths and a lie, upgraded:** a friend tells three stories; the learner asks only open and unexpected questions, then decides based on detail and consistency.
- **Myth buster:** the agent lists classic "tells"; the learner sorts them into supported, weak and myth.
