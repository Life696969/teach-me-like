# Lesson 1: The Jane look

- **The learner walks away able to:** scan a person in a fixed order and turn 10 details into 3 testable guesses.
- **Scene:** `scenes/01-pilot-kitchen-read.md`, Part A · **Technique:** `techniques/observation.md` · **About 20 minutes**
- Run the beats in order. *Wait* means stop and let the learner answer.

## 1. Hook

Ask them, without looking: what colour is the wall behind them, and what's on it? Then tell them to look. Most people get details wrong about a wall they see every day. Point: *we look all day and register almost nothing. Jane's first trick isn't magic; it's actually registering.* **Wait.**

## 2. The Jane moment

Tell Part A of scene 01 up to its stop point (Jane alone in the grieving mother's kitchen). Ask: *"He's in her kitchen with nobody watching. What would you look at?"* **Wait.** Then tell the rest of Part A: the guesses, her "are you psychic?", and his answer that he just pays attention. End with what he noticed (her home and pantry; how she stood at the press conference).

## 3. The technique: the scan

1. **Fixed order, every time:** head → face → hands → clothes → shoes → what they carry → how they move → what's around them. A fixed order stops you skipping things.
2. **Details first, conclusions later.** Say "worn heels, outer edge" before "walks a lot".
3. **Ask "what causes this?"** Wear, stains, calluses, fading, mismatches and recent changes are the best clues, because habits cause them.
4. **Give each clue two or three explanations.** A pale ring line could mean a divorce, a ring being resized, gym days or a lost ring.
5. **Look for three clues pointing the same way.** One clue is a guess. Three agreeing clues make a read.
6. **Test lightly and update.** Being wrong cheaply is part of the skill.

Example to share: on a train, a man is rehearsing under his breath, keeps checking his phone, and has a freshly ironed shirt. Three clues agree: a big moment today, maybe a presentation or an interview.

## 4. Science check

- **Works, with limits:** brief observations predict some things moderately well (Ambady & Rosenthal 1992), and strangers can judge some traits from someone's room or photos above chance (Gosling et al. 2002; Naumann et al. 2009). Moderate, not magic.
- **Attention is the real bottleneck:** about half the viewers in a famous study missed a person in a gorilla suit (Simons & Chabris 1999). A routine fixes much of this.
- **Myth:** judging character from someone's face. Face impressions are fast but mostly wrong (Todorov et al. 2015). Read behaviour and evidence, not features.
- **TV:** one glance giving a full biography with certainty. Real reads are probabilistic and often wrong at first. Even Jane misses: mention in one line that he guesses wrong about Lisbon's teenage self (scene 06, Moment B), without retelling it.

## 5. Drill: read the commuter

Give them this description, then ask for: 10 details (just details), then 3 guesses, each with the clues behind it and one other explanation. **Wait.**

> **Case A.** A man in his late twenties on a crowded local train at 8:40 a.m. Freshly ironed formal shirt, sleeves rolled once. An office lanyard tucked into his shirt pocket. A laptop bag with a faded college-fest sticker. A steel tiffin in a cloth bag. Polished shoes, but the heels are worn down on the outer edge. A thin red thread on his wrist. Earphones in, but he keeps unlocking his phone and staring at the same screen. Eyes a little red. His lips move slightly as if he's rehearsing something.

**What a strong answer contains (for your feedback; don't show it first):**
- *Details:* the ironed shirt, rolled sleeves, lanyard, college sticker, tiffin, polished shoes, worn outer heels, red thread, the phone checking, red eyes, rehearsing.
- *Guess 1:* **a big moment at work today** (a presentation, interview or review). Clues: rehearsing, the same screen again and again, a freshly ironed shirt. Alternative: learning lines for something personal, or an exam.
- *Guess 2:* **slept badly or little.** Clues: red eyes, the early commute. Alternative: allergies, screen strain.
- *Guess 3:* **graduated fairly recently, office job.** Clues: the college sticker, the lanyard. Alternative: a hand-me-down bag, or he works at a college.
- *Bonus:* worn outer heels mean he walks a lot or has an outward gait. The tiffin means food packed at home: he lives with family or cooks. The red thread *could* be religious, family or festival-related; don't over-read it.
- *Ethics point to praise:* not guessing caste, religion or income from the thread or his looks.

**Feedback:** name what they got, the one clue they missed, and one upgrade (usually "give an alternative explanation" or "look for three agreeing clues"). Offer one retry on Case B.

> **Case B (retry).** A woman in her forties at a café, 11 a.m. on a weekday. Reading glasses pushed up on her head. A stack of printed pages covered in red-pen marks. A canvas tote from an art-supply shop. Small dried paint flecks on her forearm. Her coffee is untouched and cold. She glances at the door each time it opens. A faint pale line on her ring finger, but no ring.

*Strong details:* glasses on her head, red-pen pages, the art-supply tote, paint flecks, cold untouched coffee, glancing at the door, the pale ring line. *Strong guesses:* she corrects or grades written work (red pen, stack: teacher, editor or reviewer); paints as a hobby or job (the tote, the paint flecks); she's waiting for someone (watching the door, absorbed enough to forget her coffee). **Ring line:** many explanations (resizing, removed for painting, divorce); praise them if they say *notice it, don't announce it*.

## 6. Field exercise (next 24 hours)

Pick one stranger in a public place (a café, a train, a queue). Silently note 10 details in 60 seconds, then make 3 guesses with their clues. Then do the same with a friend, and **ask the friend** whether you were right. Score hits and misses honestly.

**Ethics line:** observe to understand, not to expose. Never announce private guesses (health, relationships, money, religion, sexuality) about a real person.

## 7. Self-check

Ask all three, then correct any wrong answers.
1. Why name details before conclusions? *Because jumping to a conclusion makes you see only the clues that agree with it.*
2. When does a guess become a read? *When around three independent clues point the same way.*
3. True or false: you can read someone's character from their face. *False. Face impressions are mostly inaccurate; read behaviour and evidence.*
