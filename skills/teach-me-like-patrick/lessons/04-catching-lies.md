# Lesson 4: Catching lies, for real

- **The learner walks away able to:** use the detail rule, open and unexpected questions, and holding back what they know.
- **Scene:** `scenes/02-pilot-diary-bait.md` · **Technique:** `techniques/baselines-and-lies.md` · **About 25 minutes**

## 1. Hook: a quick myth test

Ask true or false for each, fast: **Wait.**
1. Liars avoid eye contact.
2. Looking up and to the right means someone's making something up.
3. Most people can spot a liar about 80% of the time.

All three are **false**. Eye direction didn't separate liars from truth-tellers in three studies (Wiseman et al. 2012). Across 206 studies and about 24,000 judges, people averaged about 54% accuracy, barely better than a coin flip, and experts did little better (Bond & DePaulo 2006). "So let's learn what actually works."

## 2. The Jane moment

Tell scene 02 up to its stop point (Jane mentions the diary and the search "tomorrow"). Ask: *"There is no diary. Why would Jane invent one, and what is he waiting to see?"* **Wait.** Reveal the rest. Point out: **he didn't read a single facial expression.** He created a situation where only the guilty person would *act*, and then watched actions.

## 3. The technique: four tools that beat body language

1. **The detail rule.** Judge an account by how much **specific, checkable detail** it has: who, where, when, what exactly. Truthful accounts tend to be richer in it. In a 2023 study, people's gut judgments were at chance, but people told to use *only* this cue did reliably better (Verschuere et al. 2023).
2. **Open, story-first questions.** "Walk me through your evening from the moment you left work." Don't ask yes/no questions; let the story come out in the person's own words.
3. **Unexpected questions.** Ask what a prepared story didn't rehearse: the layout ("where were you sitting?"), the order ("what happened just before that?"), sensory details ("what was playing?"). When two people lie about the same event, their answers to unexpected questions match each other less (Vrij, Leal et al. 2009).
4. **Hold back what you know.** If you have a fact, don't reveal it first. Let the story commit, then check it against your fact. That's Jane's diary, in honest form.

**Then conclude loosely:** "This doesn't add up yet; I need more information", never "you're lying".

## 4. Science check

- **Strongest evidence:** checking against evidence. Interviewers trained to hold back their evidence caught liars about 85% of the time, against about 56% for untrained ones (Hartwig et al. 2006). It only works when you actually *have* facts.
- **Good evidence:** the detail rule (Verschuere et al. 2023), and trained analysis of statement content (Oberlader et al. 2016).
- **Promising but debated:** making the story harder to tell (e.g. asking for it in reverse order) helped in some studies (Vrij et al. 2008), but how much is contested (Levine et al. 2018; Mac Giolla & Luke 2021).
- **Myths:** micro-expressions as lie detectors. They appear in about 2% of expressions, are always partial, and also show up in genuine emotions (Porter & ten Brinke 2008). Training on them didn't help (Jordan et al. 2019). "93% of communication is nonverbal" is a misquote of studies about judging like or dislike from single words.
- **Also true:** we default to believing people, and that's usually healthy, because most communication is honest (Levine 2014).

## 5. Drill

**Part 1: apply the detail rule.** Give both accounts of "Where were you last night?" and ask: which is more likely true, and why? What would they check? **Wait.**

> **Account A:** "Left office around 7:15, took the metro from Rajiv Chowk, got off at Hauz Khas, picked up momos from the stall near gate 3. They'd run out of paneer, so I got veg. Home by 8:30; my flatmate was watching the cricket."
>
> **Account B:** "Just went home after work, ate something, watched some TV and slept. Nothing special."

*Strong answer:* A has far more specific, **checkable** detail (times, stations, the stall, the flatmate), so by the detail rule it's more likely true. Check one detail: ask the flatmate, or which match was on. **Caveats to praise:** some people are naturally brief (that's their baseline); a prepared liar can add detail, which is why it must be *checkable*; B isn't proof of lying.

**Part 2: design the questions.** Scenario: your flatmate says they didn't borrow your bicycle yesterday. You noticed the tyres were muddy this morning, and it rained only in the evening. Ask them to write three questions: one **open**, one **unexpected** and one plan for **holding back** the mud. **Wait.**

*Strong answer:* open: "What did you get up to yesterday evening?" Unexpected: "Where did you leave your umbrella when you came in?" or "What was the road like when you got back?" Hold back: don't mention the mud until their account is complete, then ask how the tyres might have got muddy. **Feedback:** reward questions that invite a story; flag accusing or yes/no questions; reward a loose conclusion.

## 6. Field exercise: two truths and a lie, upgraded

With friends who are in on the game: each person tells three short stories, one of them invented. You may ask only **open** and **unexpected** questions, then decide by **detail and consistency**, not by faces. Track your accuracy over a few rounds.

**Ethics line:** this is a game with willing players. Don't interrogate your partner, family or colleagues. If you're worried someone close to you is lying, an honest, direct conversation beats any technique.

## 7. Self-check

1. What's the one rule that beat gut feeling? *Judge by how much specific, checkable detail an account has.*
2. Why hold back what you know? *So the story commits first, and you can check it against your fact.*
3. A friend looks away while answering. What does that tell you? *Nothing by itself. Compare it with their baseline, and look at what they say, not where they look.*

## 8. Cliffhanger

"You can spot a bad story now. But people only tell you real stories when they trust you. Next lesson: how Jane walked up to an armed mob boss and left with his private phone number."
