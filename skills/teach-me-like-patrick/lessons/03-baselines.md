# Lesson 3: Baselines

- **The learner walks away able to:** read a person against *themselves*, and name three innocent explanations for any change.
- **Scene:** `scenes/01-pilot-kitchen-read.md`, Part B · **Technique:** `techniques/baselines-and-lies.md` · **About 20 minutes**

## 1. Hook

"Your friend, usually the loudest at dinner, has said almost nothing tonight. What's going on?" **Wait.** Whatever they answer, ask how they'd know. The honest answer: you can't yet, but you *can* notice that it's a change, and that's where reading starts.

## 2. The Jane moment

Briefly recap Part A of scene 01 if they haven't done Lesson 1. Then tell Part B up to its stop point (the father reacts to the accusation with controlled anger, not rage). Ask: *"Jane reads this as guilt. What else could explain that reaction?"* **Wait.** Then tell how it ends: the mother shoots her husband. Jane was right about him, but his method was a guess built on *his own theory* of how innocent men behave. That's the mistake this lesson fixes.

## 3. The technique: read people against themselves

1. **Baseline first.** Watch the person while they're relaxed and talking about easy, neutral things: how fast they talk, how much they gesture, how much eye contact they make, how much detail they give and how their voice sounds. Everyone's normal is different.
2. **Then watch for change.** A sudden shift from *their* normal (going still, giving less detail, a pause before one specific answer, a change of tone) is a **flag to look closer**, not a verdict.
3. **Name three innocent explanations** for every change before you consider a guilty one: tired, embarrassed, private, grieving, scared of not being believed, someone else is in the room.
4. **Context counts.** Something that's a change in a relaxed chat may be normal in a stressful one (an exam, a meeting with the boss, a police station).
5. **Never compare someone with "how a normal person would react".** That's Jane's error. There's no standard reaction to grief, accusation or stress.

## 4. Science check

- **Works:** baselining as a habit, because it stops you misreading someone's natural style. A naturally fidgety person who fidgets tells you nothing.
- **Limits:** a change is not a lie detector. Body cues are weak signals of deception; across 158 behaviours studied, most had no link or only a weak one (DePaulo et al. 2003).
- **Confidence trap:** people trained on behavioural "symptoms" of guilt became *more confident and less accurate*, about 46% vs 56% (Kassin & Fong 1999). Jane's certainty in the pilot is exactly this trap.
- **The show's own warning:** acting on a reaction read, without evidence, led to a killing.

## 5. Drill: the baseline game

Give one scenario at a time. For each, ask: *is this a change worth noticing? And give three innocent explanations.* **Wait** after each.

**A.** Rohan normally talks fast and jokes constantly. When you ask about his weekend, he gives a two-word answer and changes the subject.
→ *A real change, worth noticing.* Innocent explanations: something sad or embarrassing happened; plans he wants to keep private; exhausted; he didn't want to talk in front of someone else there. **Best move:** later, privately, an open, kind question.

**B.** Priya always clicks her pen in meetings. In today's meeting she clicks it while presenting the quarterly numbers.
→ *Not a change.* That's her baseline. Anyone who calls this "nervous lying" has skipped baselining.

**C.** A shopkeeper who usually chats and makes eye contact looks away while telling you the price of something.
→ *A small change.* Innocent explanations: busy, checking stock in his head, uncomfortable about a price rise he didn't choose. **Best move:** don't read his face; compare the price elsewhere, because evidence beats faces.

**D.** Your cousin, whose texts are normally three words long, sends a long, detailed message explaining why she missed the wedding.
→ *A change, but notice which way it points.* Innocent explanations: she feels genuinely bad, wants to be understood, or something real and complicated happened. Teaser: research suggests truthful accounts tend to carry *more* checkable detail, not less. That's next lesson.

**Feedback:** praise innocent explanations especially. If they jump to "lying" anywhere, point back to Jane in the pilot.

## 6. Field exercise

Pick one person you'll talk to tomorrow (a friend, a colleague, family). For the first two minutes, about something easy, silently note their baseline: speed, gestures, eye contact, detail. Later in the conversation, notice one change and think of three innocent explanations. Don't act on it; just notice.

**Ethics line:** never accuse anyone based on a change. Use it to ask a kinder, better question.

## 7. Self-check

1. What's a baseline? *How this particular person behaves when relaxed and talking about neutral things.*
2. You spot a change from someone's baseline. What does it mean? *Only that something is different. Look closer and think of innocent explanations first.*
3. What was Jane's mistake with the father? *He compared the father with his own theory of how an innocent man reacts, not with the father's own baseline or with evidence.*
