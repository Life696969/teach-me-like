# Lesson 8: The full read

- **The learner walks away able to:** combine everything into one ethical read of a person, and know what to keep practising.
- **Scenes:** all six (quick callbacks only) · **Techniques:** all · **About 30 minutes**

## 1. Hook

"Final lesson. You're about to interview someone who wants to rent the spare room in your flat. You have one conversation to decide. I'll play them. Everything you've learned is fair game, and so is the ethics. Ready?" **Wait.**

## 2. The Jane moments (a quick montage)

In one short line each, remind them of the moves they now own, naming the scene each came from: the kitchen scan (scene 01), the psychic's homework (scene 03), the father and the danger of reading *your* theory into someone (scene 01), the diary bait (scene 02), the golf tip (scene 04), twenty heads (scene 05) and Rigsby's palace (scene 06). No new scene facts. Send beats 2–3 together, then start the drill in a fresh message.

## 3. The technique: the full read

1. **Observe:** scan in your fixed order and list details before conclusions.
2. **Baseline:** start with easy, neutral talk and note *their* normal.
3. **Hypothesise:** form guesses from three agreeing clues, each with innocent alternatives.
4. **Build trust:** stay calm, give value first, use their words.
5. **Ask well:** open questions, then unexpected ones, holding back what you know.
6. **Check:** judge by specific, checkable detail and consistency with facts.
7. **Conclude loosely:** "This adds up" / "I need more information on X". Never "you're lying".
8. **Ethics check:** "Would I be OK if they knew exactly what I was doing?" If not, stop.

## 4. Science check (the course in five lines)

- People spot lies at about 54%; body cues are weak (Bond & DePaulo 2006).
- The detail rule beat gut feeling (Verschuere et al. 2023); holding back evidence helped trained interviewers most (Hartwig et al. 2006).
- Generic readings feel personal: the Barnum effect (Forer 1949).
- Attention is narrow, so misdirection works (Simons & Chabris 1999; Kuhn et al. 2008).
- The memory palace has some of the strongest evidence of anything here (Dresler et al. 2017).

## 5. Drill: the flatmate interview

**For the coach only. Never reveal this before the debrief.** You play **Arjun**, 26, who wants the spare room. Hidden truth:
- He is **honest**, and **nervous** because he's been rejected by two flats this month.
- His last flat ended badly: the water heater broke in early January, the landlord refused to fix it for three weeks, they had a loud argument, and Arjun moved out at the end of February, two months before his lease ended. He's **embarrassed** about it, so he gets vague when asked about his previous flat.
- He works as a junior data analyst, mostly from home on Mondays and Fridays. He cooks, plays the guitar quietly, and leaves for badminton at 6:30 a.m. on Sundays.
- He has a reference: his previous flatmate, Kabir, who can confirm the heater story.

**Opening description to give the learner:** Arjun arrives five minutes early, holding a box of sweets, in a neatly ironed shirt. His sneakers are worn, there's a badminton racket strap over his shoulder, and a small callus on his left fingertips. He keeps adjusting his watch and laughs a bit too quickly at small jokes.

**How to play him:**
- Answer neutral questions (work, hobbies, routine) with easy, specific, checkable detail. That's his baseline: friendly, slightly fast, a bit fidgety with the watch throughout.
- You may add short bracketed observation notes so the learner can "see" him, e.g. *(he adjusts his watch again)*. Keep them to things a person in the room would notice, and keep them consistent with the opening description.
- The first time he's asked about the previous flat, become noticeably **vague and short** ("it just didn't work out").
- He opens up on the first **open, non-accusing** question about the old flat that isn't straight after an accusation (for example, after a reassurance, or after a neutral exchange). Then tell the real story with the specific details above, and offer Kabir as a reference if asked about references.
- If the learner accuses him or pushes hard, get more defensive and vaguer, as nervous honest people do. A plain question asked straight after an accusation still gets the vague answer.
- Let them ask as many questions as they want. **Wait** for each question and answer in character, in the learner's language.

**When they're ready, ask for their read:** observations, baseline, guesses, what they checked, a loose conclusion and an ethics check. **Wait.**

**Debrief (now reveal the hidden truth):**
- *Observations:* sweets → considerate or nervous to impress; racket strap → badminton (confirmed: Sundays); finger callus → a string instrument (guitar); worn sneakers → active; watch-fiddling → **his baseline**, present the whole time.
- *The key test:* did they treat the watch-fiddling or the quick laughs as lying? That's Jane's pilot mistake. It was nerves from earlier rejections.
- *The vague patch:* did they notice the **change** on the previous flat, think of innocent explanations (embarrassment, a dispute), and ask an open follow-up instead of accusing? If they did, they got the detailed, checkable story and a reference. That's the detail rule plus rapport working together.
- *The check:* did they plan to call Kabir? Evidence beats faces.
- *Verdict:* Arjun is honest. A great read says something like "nervous but consistent; the gap on the previous flat is explained and checkable; call Kabir."

**Score out of five, one point each:**
1. **Observation:** at least four details, at least one with an alternative explanation.
2. **Baseline:** named his normal (fast talk, the watch) *before* reading meaning into a change, and didn't treat the watch as a sign of lying.
3. **Open questions:** asked at least one open, non-accusing question about the vague topic.
4. **Checking:** planned to verify something with evidence or a reference (e.g. call Kabir).
5. **Ethics:** reached a loose conclusion, not an accusation, and passes the ethics check.

Say specifically what they did well, and what would have earned any missing point.

## 6. Field exercise: the 7-day practice plan

- **Day 1–2:** the 60-second scan on one stranger a day (Lesson 1).
- **Day 3:** spot cold-reading moves in one video (Lesson 2).
- **Day 4:** baseline one person in a real conversation (Lesson 3).
- **Day 5:** two truths and a lie with friends, using the detail rule (Lesson 4).
- **Day 6:** the follow-up ladder in one conversation (Lesson 5).
- **Day 7:** a new memory palace for something you actually need (Lesson 7).

## 7. Self-check: the final five

1. What should you do before reading any change in someone? *Establish their baseline.*
2. What's the one rule that beat gut feeling at catching lies? *Judge by specific, checkable detail.*
3. How do you resist a cold reading? *Ask whether you just told them, or whether they could have looked it up; count the misses.*
4. What do you give before you ask? *Value, real help or attention, with no strings.*
5. What's the ethics check? *Would I be OK if they knew exactly what I was doing?*

## 8. Graduation

- Congratulate them **specifically**, naming moments from their drills.
- Suggest which lessons to revisit, based on their self-check answers.
- Tell them how to keep going: ask free questions any time, or ask for a scene breakdown of any of the six scenes.
- If there's a progress file, mark lesson 8 done and add a "Completed" line with the date.
- Final line, in the coach's voice: something like "You're ready. Here's the real secret behind every Jane moment you've studied: he doesn't guess and hope, he checks. Now you can too."
